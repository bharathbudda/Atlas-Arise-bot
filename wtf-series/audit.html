What the big faceless channels do that our kit doesn't

I audited the seven channels you sent, plus the leading faceless Shorts formats and YouTube's rules on repetitive AI content. Three research agents gathered the material. Claims I could source are linked; my own observations and estimates are marked "est." The last sections turn the findings into a new asset system for WTF. No credits have been spent.

Why our first kit reads as amateur

  1. There is no character. Six of the seven channels put a face on screen: caricatures, a mascot, chibi figures, painted portraits, or the presenter himself. Bill wurtz is the only exception, and his voice and typography act as the character. Our kit had a palette but nobody to watch.
  2. The contrast is too low for a phone. Thin chalk lines on dark navy look muddy at Shorts size. Every reference uses thick dark outlines, saturated flat fills and huge stroked type.
  3. It has no signature device. Johnny Harris has the globe-zoom and highlighter, History on Maps the date ticker, OverSimplified the caricature reactions. A shared grid overlay alone isn't something people recognise.
  4. The pace is long-form pace. Our shots held 3.5–7 seconds. Shorts that work change the picture every 1.5–3 seconds and never sit fully still.
  5. The narration has information but no persona. The references all have attitude: deadpan understatement, second-person "you are…", wry asides, questions. Our scripts read like accurate captions.

What wins in Shorts specifically

The best Shorts benchmark: Zack D. Films (29.6M subs)

  • The title is a question plus an emoji ("What If The Hoover Dam Was Made Of Humans 😨", 11M views).
  • The first frame is already mid-action, and the camera is "almost always moving" (Skool).
  • Recurring generic characters. Captions are 1–3 words in the middle of the screen. The last line runs back into the first, so it loops est.
  • The AI clones use the same stack we have: script, Nano Banana images, ElevenLabs voice, an image-to-video model and an editor (Lilys).

Retention mechanics

  • The first 1–3 seconds decide the swipe. Creators aim for 70–80%+ "viewed" (vidIQ).
  • Since 31 Mar 2025 every replay counts as a view, so a seamless loop is free reach. Shorts under about 35 s can pass 100% average viewed (Shortimize).
  • Keep captions at least 300 px above the bottom and 120 px below the top. Captions placed too low are the most common mistake (Reap).

YouTube's "inauthentic content" rule (from 15 Jul 2025)

  • Content that is "mass-produced" or "looks like it's made with a template" can lose monetisation. That includes slideshows with minimal narrative and AI content from "generic or unoriginal templates" (YouTube Help).
  • The safe path is a distinct storyline and real educational value in each video. A shared intro and outro is explicitly allowed.
  • For us: the mascot and signature device are fine as a frame. Every episode needs its own story shape and its own visuals.

Patterns every successful channel shares

  • One persistent visual system that is cheaper and more recognisable than variety: maps, paper, a cartoon figure.
  • A recurring face the viewer reads emotion from, even when the channel is faceless.
  • An anchor the video keeps coming back to: a map, a date ticker, a counter.
  • The title promises a frame ("in 5 minutes", "ENTIRE", "What if"), and the video pays it off fast.
  • Constant small motion: pushes, ticks, wipes, jitter. The screen is never fully still for more than 2–3 seconds.
  • The voice persona carries the comedy: understatement that undercuts big events.
  • The sound has a signature: a sting, a jingle, a whoosh vocabulary.
  • No preamble. Line one is already story.
  • Simple assets, effort on script and timing. None of the cheap channels wins on rendering quality; they win on clarity and personality.

Proposed WTF system: "The Second Look"

Our brand promise is that something looks obvious until you look again. Every element below acts that out, so none of it is borrowed from one channel. It is built for 9:16 and for what AI tools do reliably.

meet Mark

1. A mascot: Mark, the question mark

Mark is a small navy figure whose head is the orange "?" from our logo. At every reveal his head snaps from "?" to "!" in a double-take. That is the logo, the mascot and the punchline in one move, and no other channel can claim it.

  • He doesn't talk. One narrator stays the voice, and a mime reads in any language.
  • I generate him once as a model sheet and a pack of about 12 poses with transparent backgrounds: pointing, shocked, magnifying lens, shrug, thumbs-up, "?" to "!" flip. Every episode reuses the same files, so his look can't drift.
  • I make one double-take clip once and reuse it in every episode. That becomes the brand moment and costs nothing after the first time.
  • Sketch only: the final design comes from the character sheet.

2. The Second-Look Lens

At the twist, the frame freezes and desaturates and a "Wait." sting hits. Mark slides an orange-rimmed magnifying lens across the frame, and inside the lens is the truth: the hippo's reflection is a whale, a "square" window turns out rounded. I build it locally from two images, the surface view and the true view, so it costs no extra credits per use. It is the channel's signature device.

3. A brighter notebook

We keep the notebook idea but switch to a light page: white graph paper with a pale blue grid, an orange margin line, thick navy ink outlines, flat saturated fills, and orange highlighter for emphasis. Dark navy is kept for space and night scenes. This fixes the phone contrast problem and keeps our colours.

4. Case-file props

Every number arrives as a taped-on index card. Every source gets a stamped "SOURCE" tag. Key words get a highlighter wipe, and the things that matter get orange marker circles and arrows. All of it is built locally, so no AI-made text ever appears. It is the kind of tactile layer Johnny Harris uses, done in our own vocabulary.

5. An anchor per episode

Each episode gets one persistent on-screen counter or map that it keeps returning to, the job History on Maps' date ticker does. EP01 has a microseconds counter, EP04 a bacteria-vs-cells scoreboard, EP05 a year ticker from 1839, EP06 a flight-cycle counter, EP02 the family tree, EP03 the 12-key strip.

New rules for script and edit

Length
35–50 s instead of 52–57 s. Under about 35 s, loops can push retention past 100%. The richest topics (EP02, EP03) stay near 50 s.
Pace
Ainsley at about 165–175 wpm (up from 155), so 100–130 words per episode.
Hook (0–1.5 s)
The first frame is already mid-action, with the claim on screen and spoken. Hook shapes rotate across episodes: claim, "you" POV, myth-bust, question. That keeps the series from looking templated.
The "Wait." beat
The narrator interrupts herself at the twist ("Wait."), followed by the lens and Mark's double-take. The full sign-off becomes a 0.6 s sting instead of a 2.5 s end card.
Loop ending
The last line is written to run back into the first line.
Persona
Wry and plain-spoken, with one deadpan aside per episode ("Van Buren lost. The word won."). The accuracy rules from the fact check stay exactly as they are.
Picture rate
A new framing every 1.5–3 s, about 20–25 per episode. Each AI image yields 2–3 framings (wide, push, detail crop), plus Mark cut-ins and local cards. That keeps the image count affordable.
Motion
Nothing is ever fully still: slow pushes, a 2 px "boil" jitter on the art, highlighter wipes, cards dropping in.
Sound
Our own sting vocabulary: a pencil scribble, a tape rip, a stamp thunk, and the "Wait." hit, all synthesised locally. The brand music bed sits under every episode.
Covers
2–4 huge stroked words, one in orange; Mark reacting; one hero object with an orange circle or X.

What we take from each channel (in our own form)

  • OverSimplified: reduce famous things to one trademark feature; deadpan narration.
  • bill wurtz: no preamble, a sting between beats.
  • Johnny Harris: a tactile paper layer, highlighter, a marker circle on the key detail.
  • History on Maps: a persistent anchor counter.
  • Unknown Frequencies: second-person POV hooks, thick outlines.
  • Easy History: a mascot that physically reacts to the reveal.
  • Zack D.: a mid-action first frame and loop endings.

What we deliberately don't copy

  • No caricatures of real people, and no photo heads on cartoon bodies. Both carry likeness risk and both belong to OverSimplified.
  • No AI-generated documents, maps with labels, or archival look-alikes. Easy History's garbled AI text is the failure we're avoiding.
  • No crude-on-purpose look. Bill wurtz pulls it off with music and timing; with AI art it reads as a mistake.
  • No identical structure every episode, which is what YouTube's inauthentic-content rule targets.
